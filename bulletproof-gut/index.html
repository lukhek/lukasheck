The foundation

Gut health has many moving parts.

Two people can have almost the same symptoms and completely different causes. What helps one, such as more fibre, can make things worse for the other. That is why there is no standard plan. We look closely, observe how you react and adjust.

Good digestion starts before the gut. The whole digestive tract works as one connected system.

Deep diveProtection and repair mechanisms
Intestinal stem cells allow the lining to renew constantly. Many intestinal epithelial cells are replaced within a few days.
Paneth cells produce the body's own antimicrobial substances and help regulate the microbial balance in the small intestine.
Gut barrier controls which substances are absorbed and which stay out.

All of this works closely with the immune system. Alcohol, chronic inflammation, undernutrition and excessive strain can impair it.

Deep diveButyrate and co.: what gut bacteria make for us

Gut bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, acetate and propionate, among other things when fermenting certain fibres. Butyrate is an important energy source for the cells of the colon lining and supports the gut barrier and immune regulation. That is why a varied, individually tolerated diet is the long-term goal. With a low gut level it can make sense to temporarily reduce certain fermentable foods and bring them back later.

Deep diveBuilding blocks: protein and amino acids

For repair and renewal the gut needs enough energy, protein and amino acids. Glutamine fuels certain gut and immune cells, glycine and proline are building blocks of collagen and connective tissue, cysteine is a precursor of the antioxidant glutathione. Animal foods often make supply straightforward, but a well-planned plant-based diet can cover the need too. What counts is enough, well-tolerated supply, not the dietary philosophy.

Eating in practice

Healthy doesn't automatically mean well tolerated.

An example that surprises many: carrots, kiwi and oranges are tolerated much better by many people with a sensitive gut than, say, blackberries or apples, even though all of them are equally “healthy”.

The reason is FODMAPs: certain short-chain carbohydrates and sugar alcohols that are poorly absorbed and fermented by gut bacteria. In sensitive people this can contribute to bloating, abdominal pain or diarrhoea. A low-FODMAP diet is best studied in irritable bowel syndrome. In SIBO it can ease symptoms, but it has not been shown to clear the overgrowth itself.

This is a guide, not a fixed rule. Tolerance is individual and depends on portion size.

The amount makes the difference.

A food can be fine in a normal portion and cause symptoms in a very large one. Several individually tolerated foods can add up to too much together. This is called FODMAP stacking.

A tool for a limited time.

A stricter low-FODMAP phase is time-limited. Usually a structured reintroduction follows after two to six weeks.

Cooked instead of raw.

Cooking, steaming and braising often make food easier to digest. For a while, white rice or light sourdough bread can also make more sense than lots of wholegrain. The long-term benefits of wholegrain remain.

Long-term orientation

Long-term mindset: Paleo-Mediterranean

A flexible blend of Paleo and Mediterranean eating works well as orientation: nutrient-dense, mostly minimally processed and adjustable to you. Deliberately not strict. Rice, buckwheat, dairy or legumes can have their place, and so can the occasional sweet.

Rhythm and lifestyle

Your gut has an internal clock too.

Digestion, gut movement, hormones and metabolism follow a daily rhythm. Late, large meals and constant snacking are unfavourable for some people. A simple lever: give your gut a longer break at night.

The 12:12 strategy

7 am–7 pm: eating window
7 pm–7 am: eating break

If you usually have dinner at 7 pm and snack until 9 pm, your first experiment is to skip the snacking after dinner.

The small intestine also keeps moving between meals: the so-called migrating motor complex (MMC) supports the onward transport of leftovers and secretions during eating breaks.

Rhythm over rigidity.

You don't have to keep this to the minute every day. A late protein shake, for example, can make sense for an athlete if it is well tolerated.

Deep diveGut motility: why movement in the gut matters

Gut motility is the movement that mixes and moves along the gut contents. Not only the microbiome counts, but also how well this transport works. Slowed transport can promote constipation, impaired small-intestine movement can be a risk factor for SIBO, and transport that is too fast can contribute to diarrhoea. Regular movement, enough fluids, adequate nutrition and a fitting meal rhythm support normal function.

Deep diveStress, nervous system and connection

Gut and brain communicate constantly. Psychological stress can change gut movement, pain sensitivity and digestion. Helpful: calm meals without distraction, walks in nature, conscious breathing, meditation, regular sleep times and recovery. Important: don't turn every health measure into another performance task.

Persistent loneliness can also be a burden. In a large observational study of almost 400,000 people, loneliness was linked to a 46% higher relative risk of irritable bowel syndrome, and a smaller study found a link with lower microbiome diversity. This does not prove a direct cause and effect. Shared meals, walks with friends and trusting relationships are still sensible building blocks. Sources: Zhou et al., 2026; Nguyen et al., 2021.

Deep diveStrength training and the right dose

Strength training is an important part of long-term health. With pronounced gut problems, though, your total load should be taken into account, especially with inflammatory bowel disease. That doesn't mean giving up training. It can make sense to temporarily reduce volume, not train to failure all the time, plan more recovery and prioritise moderate movement.

What about supplements?

Supplements can support basic supply and certain body functions. But they replace neither a root-cause analysis nor an individual strategy. Which ones make sense for you and at what dose, we work out personally with you. That is why you will deliberately not find blanket recommendations here.

The goal

A Bulletproof Gut.

A resilient gut is not one that requires you to eat perfectly. It copes as well as possible with different foods, occasional deviations, stress and physical strain.

We don't want to eliminate as many foods as possible or take as many supplements as possible. We want to strengthen your gut's self-regulation and resilience.
