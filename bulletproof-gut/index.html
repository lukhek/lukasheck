The foundation

Your gut is more than a digestive organ.

It is in constant exchange with your immune system, your nervous system, your metabolism and even your mind. Gut health is multifactorial, and that is exactly the opportunity: there are many ways to influence it positively.

Everything is connected. What you do for sleep, mental health, fitness or stress can indirectly help your gut too, and the other way round. That is why we don't look only at your gut but at you as a whole system.

Biology

Discover the fascinating world of your gut.

You don't need to understand any of this in detail to improve your gut health. If you are curious, here are some especially interesting connections.

MicrobiomeYour personal microbial ecosystem

Your gut is home to trillions of microorganisms. They produce metabolites, interact with your immune system and compete for space and nutrients. What matters is not the number of good or bad bacteria but how the whole ecosystem works together.

Paneth cellsYour own antimicrobial defence

These cells sit in the crypts of the small intestine and release antimicrobial substances such as defensins and lysozyme. They help keep the balance of bacteria at the gut wall.

Goblet cellsYour biological protective layer

Goblet cells make the mucus that coats the gut wall. In the colon it is mainly the inner mucus layer that keeps bacteria at a distance from the gut wall.

E-cadherinWhat holds your gut cells together

This protein mechanically links neighbouring gut cells. It helps keep the lining stable and renew itself in an orderly way.

Tight junctionsControlled permeability

These sealing proteins between gut cells help decide what passes through and what doesn't. The gut barrier is therefore not simply tight or leaky but finely regulated. In some conditions this regulation can be disturbed.

Enteroendocrine cellsYour gut as a hormone organ

Specialised cells in the gut wall release hormones and messengers that influence satiety, digestion and gut movement. A large part of the body's serotonin is also made in the gut.

Intestinal stem cellsRemarkable regenerative capacity

The gut lining renews itself constantly. Stem cells in the crypts keep producing new cells, so the lining of the small intestine is largely renewed within a few days.

Gut-associated immune systemWhere defence meets tolerance

A large part of your immune system sits in the gut. It has to fend off pathogens while tolerating harmless food and useful bacteria. This balance is crucial.

The enteric nervous systemYour second brain

It consists of hundreds of millions of nerve cells in the gut wall. It controls movement and secretion largely on its own and is in constant contact with the brain via the vagus nerve.

Migrating motor complex (MMC)The self-cleaning system

Between meals, waves of strong contractions run through the small intestine and move residue and secretions along. Eating interrupts these waves. Impaired activity is linked, among other things, to SIBO.

Butyrate and bacterial metabolitesWhat your bacteria produce for you

Certain bacteria turn fibre into short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate. Butyrate is an important energy source for the cells of the colon and supports the gut barrier. It also shows that not every gut copes equally well with every amount of fibre.

Stomach acid, bile and digestive enzymesWhy gut problems can start higher up

Stomach acid is a first protective barrier against germs, while bile and digestive enzymes prepare food. If this pre-digestion doesn't work well, the environment in the gut can change too.

Biofilms, efflux pumps and resistanceWhy microorganisms are so adaptable

Bacteria have various survival mechanisms, including protective biofilms and transport proteins that move certain substances out of the cell. Antibiotic resistance shows how adaptable microorganisms can be. How much individual mechanisms matter for individual symptoms is not always clear.

The gut–brain axisWhy your mind plays a role

Stress, emotional strain and possibly social isolation can influence gut function through neural, hormonal and immunological pathways. Conversely, persistent gut symptoms can weigh on mental health.

Circadian biologyYour gut has an internal clock too

Digestion, gut movement, metabolism and the microbiome fluctuate over the day. Regular daily routines and mealtimes can help support these biological rhythms.

Individual

Why your gut health is individual.

Two people can suffer from the same symptoms and still have completely different causes.

Example cause

Same symptoms. Different causes. Possibly different interventions.

Conversely, a single underlying disturbance can cause many different symptoms. That is why blanket recommendations often miss the mark: what works brilliantly for one person can do nothing for another or even make symptoms worse.

Lifestyle

Your lifestyle is part of the solution.

Gut health doesn't begin on your plate or with a supplement.

Movement and nature

Regular movement can support gut motility. Time in nature can also help regulate stress.

Sleep and body clock

Your gut follows biological rhythms. Regular sleep and meal times can support them.

Mental health

Anything that does you good mentally can potentially benefit your gut too, via the gut–brain axis.

Social connection

Relationships, loneliness and emotional strain can also affect your health through stress mechanisms.

A simple strategy: 12:12

8 am–8 pm: eating window
8 pm–8 am: eating break

Twelve hours for eating, twelve hours of rest, mostly overnight. It is not an extreme fasting protocol but a simple way to give digestion more structure. A tool, not an obligation, and not equally suitable for everyone.

Rhythm over rigidity.

You don't have to keep this to the minute every day. A late protein shake, for example, can make sense for an athlete if it is well tolerated.

Deep diveStress, nervous system and connection

Gut and brain communicate constantly. Psychological stress can change gut movement, pain sensitivity and digestion. Helpful: calm meals without distraction, walks in nature, conscious breathing, meditation, regular sleep times and recovery. Important: don't turn every health measure into another performance task.

Persistent loneliness can also be a burden. In a large observational study of almost 400,000 people, loneliness was linked to a 46% higher relative risk of irritable bowel syndrome, and a smaller study found a link with lower microbiome diversity. This does not prove a direct cause and effect. Shared meals, walks with friends and trusting relationships are still sensible building blocks. Sources: Zhou et al., 2026; Nguyen et al., 2021.

Deep diveStrength training and the right dose

Strength training is an important part of long-term health. With pronounced gut problems, though, your total load should be taken into account, especially with inflammatory bowel disease. That doesn't mean giving up training. It can make sense to temporarily reduce volume, not train to failure all the time, plan more recovery and prioritise moderate movement.

Nutrition

Nutrition: tools, not dogmas.

Nutrition is individual and always more than macros and gut. It is also enjoyment, culture, company and quality of life. That is why you will find here a collection of nutrition approaches and philosophies we can use to support your gut. Not all are equally well studied or equally healthy in the long run. What matters is what makes sense in your situation, not tying you to one philosophy for good.

Healthy doesn't automatically mean well tolerated.

An example that surprises many: carrots, kiwi and oranges are tolerated much better by many people with a sensitive gut than, say, blackberries or apples, even though all of them are equally “healthy”.

Long-termMediterranean

A well-studied way of eating built on plant variety, olive oil, fish and nutrient-dense foods.

Personal choiceVegetarian / vegan

Can work well and is a conscious choice for many. Protein supply and certain micronutrients need particular attention. Vitamin B12 must be supplemented reliably.

OrientationPaleo-oriented

Can help simplify eating and cut back on highly processed food.

MindsetPaleo-Mediterranean

A flexible blend of Paleo and Mediterranean eating works well as orientation: nutrient-dense, mostly minimally processed and adjustable to you. Deliberately not strict. Rice, buckwheat, dairy or legumes can have their place, and so can the occasional sweet.

TemporaryLow FODMAP

If certain foods regularly trigger symptoms, it can make sense to reduce them first or leave them out for a while. It is no coincidence that many people with irritable bowel symptoms experience certain high-FODMAP foods as triggers. Low FODMAP is mainly a tool for temporary relief and for identifying individual triggers, not for avoiding everything fermentable for good.

The reason is FODMAPs: certain short-chain carbohydrates and sugar alcohols that are poorly absorbed and fermented by gut bacteria. In sensitive people this can contribute to bloating, abdominal pain or diarrhoea. A low-FODMAP diet is best studied in irritable bowel syndrome. In SIBO it can ease symptoms, but it has not been shown to clear the overgrowth itself.

This is a guide, not a fixed rule. Tolerance is individual and depends on portion size.

The amount makes the difference.

A food can be fine in a normal portion and cause symptoms in a very large one. Several individually tolerated foods can add up to too much together. This is called FODMAP stacking.

A tool for a limited time.

A stricter low-FODMAP phase is time-limited. Usually a structured reintroduction follows after two to six weeks.

Cooked instead of raw.

Cooking, steaming and braising often make food easier to digest. For a while, white rice or light sourdough bread can also make more sense than lots of wholegrain. The long-term benefits of wholegrain remain.

Further reading: Monash University – FODMAP Diet

TemporaryModerately fewer carbohydrates

Can help some people lower the overall fermentable load. A ketogenic diet is not needed for that. We look first at type and tolerance of carbohydrates, then at the amount.

Short testLow-fibre test

A way to observe how much plant-based, fermentable components affect your symptoms.

Only after talking to us: As a short self-experiment, some people report trying a day of animal foods only (eggs, fish, meat). It can give hints, but it is neither a SIBO test nor proof that plant foods are harmful, and with constipation it can backfire. An animal-only diet is not an established treatment and not a long-term solution. Please speak to us first.

Get to know low FODMAP

Help me get to know a practical low-FODMAP way of eating. Follow the recommendations of Monash University and recognised gastroenterology societies. Check your statements against reliable sources and link them. Create a clear mini food list, sorted by protein, carbohydrates, vegetables, fruit, fats and dairy or alternatives, with concrete portion sizes. Ask me first about my preferences and known intolerances. Then create three simple, high-protein recipe ideas: a first meal of the day, a cold main meal and a warm main meal. Briefly explain why low FODMAP should not be a permanent elimination diet. Flag uncertainties and don't invent anything.

A day to try

Based on low FODMAP, suggest one breakfast, one warm lunch and one sweet meal. Everything practical, ready in 20 minutes at most, with ingredients and amounts, without garlic and onions.

Beyond nutrition

Why we think beyond nutrition.

Especially with long-standing gut symptoms, changing your diet alone is often not enough. Research on the microbiome, gut barrier and immune system opens up many more starting points. That is why, alongside nutrition and lifestyle, we also work with carefully chosen supplements and other interventions. Which ones make sense for you, we work out personally with you.

We pay particular attention to:

  • A good risk–benefit ratio
  • Your individual starting point
  • Scientific evidence and practical experience
  • A sensible combination and dosage
  • As little unnecessary effort for you as possible
We cannot guarantee an improvement. But we can work systematically on improving the conditions for it. Persistent symptoms should also be checked by a doctor.
To take with you

At a glance.

  • Gut health has many causes. Blanket tips don't fit every gut.
  • Restriction is a tool for a while, not a permanent state.
  • Healthy doesn't automatically mean well tolerated.
  • With a sensitive gut, cooked food is often easier to digest than raw.
  • Type, amount and timing matter more than single foods.
